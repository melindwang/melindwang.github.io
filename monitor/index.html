Is AI debt building toward a financial crisis?

AI companies are committing enormous sums to data centers and computing infrastructure before we know whether the returns will justify the cost. I built this monitor to follow how that investment is financed and who would absorb the losses if it falls short. I look at how losses in one part of the economy can propagate to the rest of the financial system.

What can debt and asset prices tell us?

A rise in prices becomes more concerning when borrowing rises alongside it. Greenwood and coauthors studied 42 countries from 1950 to 2016. When business debt grew faster than in four-fifths of past periods and stock prices rose faster than in two-thirds, a financial crisis followed within three years 45% of the time. Who would absorb an AI loss?

An investment can lose value without causing a financial crisis. What matters is who holds it, how much they borrowed to buy it, and what they do when its value falls. The dot-com crash and the housing crisis are useful comparisons because losses passed through the financial system differently. I want to understand those differences before assuming that an AI correction would follow either path.

The table follows the main ways AI investment is financed. For each, I ask who would take the loss, what could force them to sell assets or stop lending, and how that might affect banks and dealers. Many of the available measures cover whole markets, so they give us context without isolating AI exposure.

A fund that locks up its investors’ money for years has more time to absorb losses than a lender that needs to refinance next month. Insurers and funds differ on this point. Some annuities can be surrendered, some insurers issue notes that mature, and some funds allow limited withdrawals or borrow from banks. Guarantees affect who is protected, but I also need to know how quickly funding can leave and what would have to be sold to repay it.

This is where I still have the most work to do. The table identifies possible connections, but I can’t yet use it to estimate how much of an AI investment loss would reach bank capital.

If losses reach banks, how do they spread?

I built this model by combining my own mathematical work on dynamic bifurcation theory and coupled economic systems with established models in economics, particularly Brunnermeier and Sannikov’s (2011) model of financial intermediaries. I want to understand when a loss changes the behavior of the system as a whole.

The model connects banks’ decisions with how markets learn about risk. Banks respond to what they believe, but their decisions also change the prices they observe. That feedback can allow an initial loss to develop into a persistent crisis. The stress tests below start by assuming that losses have reached banks; the question of whether AI losses would reach them remains open.

  1. Step 1

    Banks lose capital

    The stress test begins with an assumed loss to bank and dealer capital.

  2. Step 2

    Asset sales deepen the losses

    In the model, the loss of capital leads banks and dealers to sell assets. The resulting price declines reduce their capital further.

  3. Step 3

    Markets revise their view of risk

    Markets learn from these price movements and come to see assets as riskier. Their response lowers prices further, even though underlying risk hasn’t changed.

The starting parameters come from Brunnermeier and Sannikov’s worked example. These scenarios let us explore how the mechanism behaves under different assumptions. Neither is an estimate of losses from AI or a reconstruction of 2008.

The outcome can change abruptly

For smaller losses, bank equity recovers. Increase the loss just beyond a certain point and equity remains low. This is what interests me about dynamic bifurcation: a small change in a parameter can produce a qualitatively different outcome for the whole system.

Ma and Wang’s dynamic transition theory describes this kind of change as a catastrophic transition.

A long calm changes the starting conditions

During a long calm, markets come to see assets as safer. Banks then hold less capital and borrow more. When a loss eventually arrives, it meets a financial system that has become more vulnerable to it.

Brunnermeier and Sannikov call this the volatility paradox.

Banks help create the risk they learn from

The feedback continues after losses begin. Banks sell assets, prices move, and markets interpret those movements as evidence of greater risk. Their response creates further price movements. In the coupled model, this can keep the economy in a crisis state.

In my coupled framework, the interaction between optimization and learning can sustain a second, self-confirming crisis state.

Why track bank stocks?

To evaluate the monitor, I need an outcome that I can define before I know what happens. I track a 30% decline in bank stocks. It gives me a way to follow stress at banks while I work on the broader question of who would absorb losses from AI investment.

Debt builds upMortgages, data centers, commercial property
Some of it goes badThe holders of the debt absorb the losses
Does it reach banks?Possible routes include direct holdings, lending to holders, and contractual obligations
Bank and dealer capital fallsAsset sales and changing beliefs can deepen losses
Bank stocks fall 30%The event this monitor tracks

The threshold comes from Baron, Verner and Xiong’s study of 46 countries since 1870. They found that bank-stock declines of this size were followed by real GDP about 3% lower three years later, even without a run on deposits. It is a useful outcome to track, but it won’t capture every costly AI correction. A 30% decline by itself also doesn’t establish that the whole financial system is in crisis.

Does this depend on AI being a bubble?

I don’t need to settle that question to study the financing. AI could change the economy and still disappoint some of the investors paying for it. What matters here is how those losses would be absorbed. Jordà, Schularick and Taylor’s study of 17 countries over 140 years found that bubbles financed with credit, particularly housing, were followed by deeper recessions and more frequent financial crises. That gives us a reason to examine the borrowing behind AI, while leaving open how a correction would actually unfold.

About the monitor

  • Each week, I compare public data with U.S. history since 1955.
  • I combine my mathematical work on economic transitions with established financial models, and check the implementation against their published results.
  • Keeping the weekly record lets us assess whether these readings tell us anything useful over time.

What I’m still working on

  • The monitor does not yet provide validated crisis probabilities. It is not investment advice.
  • I’m tracing how losses on AI investment could reach banks through direct lending, other lenders, and asset prices.
  • The interactive model uses a worked example. The technical note also maps current bank capital into the model, but that exercise still needs validation and covers only part of AI financing.
  • Debt data arrive about six months late and can be revised. The dates beside the readings matter.
