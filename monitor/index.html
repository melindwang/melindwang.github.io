Is AI debt building toward a financial crisis?

AI companies are committing enormous sums to data centers and computing infrastructure before we know whether the returns will justify the cost. I built this monitor to follow how that investment is financed and who would absorb the losses if it falls short. I look at how losses in one part of the economy can propagate to the rest of the financial system.

A rise in prices becomes more concerning when borrowing rises alongside it. Greenwood and coauthors studied 42 countries from 1950 to 2016. When business debt grew faster than in four-fifths of past periods and stock prices rose faster than in two-thirds, a financial crisis followed within three years 45% of the time. Who would absorb an AI loss?

An investment can lose value without causing a financial crisis. What matters is who holds it, how much they borrowed to buy it, and what they do when its value falls. The dot-com crash and the housing crisis are useful comparisons because losses passed through the financial system differently. I want to understand those differences before assuming that an AI correction would follow either path.

The table follows the main ways AI investment is financed. For each, I ask who would take the loss, what could force them to sell assets or stop lending, and how that might affect banks and dealers. Many of the available measures cover whole markets, so they give us context without isolating AI exposure.

A fund that locks up its investors’ money for years has more time to absorb losses than a lender that needs to refinance next month. Insurers and funds differ on this point. Some annuities can be surrendered, some insurers issue notes that mature, and some funds allow limited withdrawals or borrow from banks. Guarantees affect who is protected, but I also need to know how quickly funding can leave and what would have to be sold to repay it.

This is where I still have the most work to do. The table identifies possible connections, but I can’t yet use it to estimate how much of an AI investment loss would reach bank capital.

If losses reach banks, how do they spread?

I built this model by combining my own mathematical work on dynamic bifurcation theory and coupled economic systems with established models in economics, particularly Brunnermeier and Sannikov’s (2011) model of financial intermediaries. I want to understand when a loss changes the behavior of the system as a whole.

The model connects banks’ decisions with how markets learn about risk. Banks respond to what they believe, but their decisions also change the prices they observe. That feedback can allow an initial loss to develop into a persistent crisis. The stress tests below start by assuming that losses have reached banks; the question of whether AI losses would reach them remains open.

  1. Step 1

    Banks lose capital

    The stress test begins with an assumed loss to bank and dealer capital.

  2. Step 2

    Asset sales deepen the losses

    In the model, the loss of capital leads banks and dealers to sell assets. The resulting price declines reduce their capital further.

  3. Step 3

    Markets revise their view of risk

    Markets learn from these price movements and come to see assets as riskier. Their response lowers prices further, even though underlying risk hasn’t changed.

The starting parameters come from Brunnermeier and Sannikov’s worked example. These scenarios let us explore how the mechanism behaves under different assumptions. Neither is an estimate of losses from AI or a reconstruction of 2008.

The outcome can change abruptly

For smaller losses, bank equity recovers. Increase the loss just beyond a certain point and equity remains low. This is what interests me about dynamic bifurcation: a small change in a parameter can produce a qualitatively different outcome for the whole system.

Ma and Wang’s dynamic transition theory describes this kind of change as a catastrophic transition.

A long calm changes the starting conditions

During a long calm, markets come to see assets as safer. Banks then hold less capital and borrow more. When a loss eventually arrives, it meets a financial system that has become more vulnerable to it.

Brunnermeier and Sannikov call this the volatility paradox.

Banks help create the risk they learn from

The feedback continues after losses begin. Banks sell assets, prices move, and markets interpret those movements as evidence of greater risk. Their response creates further price movements. In the coupled model, this can keep the economy in a crisis state.

In my coupled framework, the interaction between optimization and learning can sustain a second, self-confirming crisis state.

Why track bank stocks?

To evaluate the monitor, I need an outcome that I can define before I know what happens. I track a 30% decline in bank stocks. It gives me a way to follow stress at banks while I work on the broader question of who would absorb losses from AI investment.

Bank stocks fall 30%The event this monitor tracks

The threshold comes from Baron, Verner and Xiong’s study of 46 countries since 1870. They found that bank-stock declines of this size were followed by real GDP about 3% lower three years later, even without a run on deposits. It is a useful outcome to track, but it won’t capture every costly AI correction. A 30% decline by itself also doesn’t establish that the whole financial system is in crisis.

Does this depend on AI being a bubble?

I don’t need to settle that question to study the financing. AI could change the economy and still disappoint some of the investors paying for it. What matters here is how those losses would be absorbed. Jordà, Schularick and Taylor’s study of 17 countries over 140 years found that bubbles financed with credit, particularly housing, were followed by deeper recessions and more frequent financial crises. That gives us a reason to examine the borrowing behind AI, while leaving open how a correction would actually unfold.

  • Keeping the weekly record lets us assess whether these readings tell us anything useful over time.

Technical note: the data and the model

Week of October 26, 2026. Melinda L. Wang.

I built this monitor to connect a question about AI financing with a question about economic dynamics: who would absorb an investment loss, and when could their response change the behavior of the wider financial system? This note explains the data, the model, and what I can currently infer from each.

The conditions rating this week is Moderate. Business and household credit relative to GDP have fallen by 6.7 and 6.4 percentage points over three years, while real stock prices are up 55%. The stock-price threshold is crossed, but the credit threshold is not. Bank stocks are 11.0% below their August high, compared with the 30% decline I track.

For the model, I bring together my own work on dynamic bifurcation theory and coupled economic systems with Brunnermeier and Sannikov’s (2011) financial economy. The extension allows markets to learn about risk from prices that their own decisions help produce. The simulations show how this interaction can sustain a second crisis state. Establishing how well that mechanism describes current conditions is still part of the research.

The thresholds are the 80th percentile of the U.S. credit series and the 67th percentile of the U.S. price series. At each date, I calculate them using observations from 1955 through that date.

How I construct the indicator

I follow Greenwood, Hanson, Shleifer and Sørensen (2022) in pairing business credit with stock prices and household credit with house prices. The credit measure is the three-year change in credit relative to GDP; the price measure is three-year growth after inflation. A pair enters the shaded area when credit growth is in the top quintile and price growth is in the top tercile.

There are two differences from their study. I calculate percentiles from U.S. observations, rather than their pooled sample of 42 countries, and expand the threshold window through each reading date. Their 45% crisis frequency therefore cannot be read as a probability for this indicator. The historical replay also needs a qualification: thresholds exclude later observations, but the earlier series use their current revised values. Weekly data vintages are archived from the first automated run onward. Earlier releases can be reconstructed from ALFRED for back-testing with a FRED API key.

Using these date-specific thresholds, the business pair enters the zone in 1973 Q2, 1984 Q3 to 1988 Q4, 1998 Q3 to 2000 Q4, 2015 Q2 to 2016 Q1, and 2020 Q3 to 2021 Q3. The household pair enters in 1986 Q1 to 1988 Q4 and 2000 Q4 to 2007 Q1. The 2020–21 entries largely reflect the fall in nominal GDP, which raised the debt-to-GDP ratios. These episodes describe the indicator’s history; there are too few U.S. crises to estimate a reliable hit rate.

AI commitments. I also ask how the chart would change if some commitments were included alongside reported debt. Data-center lease commitments at five hyperscalers rose by about $1.0 trillion over three years, equivalent to 3.1 percentage points of GDP. Treating all $3.1 trillion in off-balance-sheet commitments at nine AI firms as new debt would add 9.5 points. Even on that assumption, business debt growth would be +2.8 points, below the +5.1-point threshold. This is an arithmetic sensitivity case: the commitments are not all loans, and adding them does not measure bank exposure. The figures come from an advocacy report’s compilation of company filings.

The XBRL filings available this week provide a separate set of measures: $424 billion in long-term debt at 9 firms, up 113% over a year; $283 billion in future operating-lease payments at 9 firms, up 42%; and $678 billion in purchase obligations at 7 firms, up 534%. Capital spending over the trailing twelve months was $262 billion, against operating cash flow of $698 billion. These measures differ in coverage and definition, so I report them separately.

Conditions rating

The rating counts threshold crossings within each sector. Low means none are crossed. Moderate means at least one is crossed, but neither sector crosses both. High means both thresholds are crossed in one sector; Very high means they are crossed in both. This week, only the stock-price threshold is crossed, giving a Moderate rating. AI commitments, bank-stock declines, and private-credit measures provide additional context but do not set that rating.

From 1985 Q1 to 2025 Q4, the rating was Low in 29 quarters, Moderate in 76, High in 46, and Very high in 13. It reached High or Very high before the savings-and-loan crisis (1985–88), the dot-com crash (1998–2000), and the 2008 crisis (the household pair, 2000–07). It also reached those levels in 2015–16 and 2020–21 without a subsequent crisis. The rating orders the observed conditions; it has not been mapped to a crisis probability.

The debt and price measures give context for the model’s leverage and risk assumptions. In section 3, lower capital at the payout boundary \(\eta^*\) and higher \(\psi q/\eta\) correspond to greater leverage, while \(\hat\sigma\) describes perceived risk. The indicator does not identify the learning speed \(\gamma\) or the size and location of a loss \(\ell\). Those require separate assumptions or measurements.

Tracing who would absorb the losses

A loss of market value does not tell us how much financial disruption will follow. We also need to know who holds the claim, how they financed it, and whether they can continue holding it after a loss. The dot-com crash and the mortgage crisis illustrate different financing structures. In 2007–08, banks and dealers held mortgage-backed securities financed with short-term debt, exposing leveraged intermediaries directly to the losses (Brunnermeier, 2009). Jordà, Schularick and Taylor (2015) find, across 17 advanced economies and 140 years, that credit-financed bubbles were followed by deeper recessions and more frequent financial crises than other bubbles.

That evidence motivates the loss map, but it doesn’t establish how an AI correction would unfold. The model in sections 2 to 4 starts with a loss at intermediaries. The table below asks how such a loss might get there: who holds each claim, what could force them to sell or reduce lending, and which exposures connect them to banks and dealers.

Funding can leave an institution even when its liabilities have no deposit guarantee. Some annuities allow surrender, usually subject to a charge. Some insurers also issue funding-agreement-backed notes that need to be repaid or refinanced, or borrow through Federal Home Loan Bank advances. Private-credit funds differ too: many lock up investors’ capital, while others allow limited quarterly redemptions and borrow from banks. These differences affect how quickly an institution might need to raise cash.

Insurance guarantees answer a related question: which policyholder claims remain protected after an insurer fails? State life and health guaranty associations, coordinated by NOLHGA, provide coverage subject to statutory limits. Annuity coverage is commonly $250,000 in present value per owner and insurer, though limits vary by state. Assessments on surviving insurers fund the associations, and premium-tax offsets can pass part of that cost to state revenue. This differs from federally backed FDIC insurance, which covers deposits up to $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category.

I therefore look at both the protection attached to a liability and the timing of possible withdrawals. The Federal Reserve’s May 2026 Financial Stability Report judged the risks from further private-credit redemptions to be limited and manageable. That assessment is useful context, rather than a reason to treat all private-credit vehicles as having the same funding structure.

In discussions of Apollo and Athene, I find it useful to separate policyholder protection from a guarantee of the firm. Guaranty associations protect eligible policyholder claims within state limits; they do not guarantee Apollo’s equity or every obligation of Athene. Premium-tax offsets can create a fiscal cost without amounting to a blanket bailout. Shareholders and creditors can still bear losses, depending on the entity and the priority of their claims.

For this monitor, the next question is how stress at an insurer could reach banks. Possible connections include loans and other contractual exposures, funding through the Federal Home Loan Banks, and changes in the prices of assets held by both institutions. The existence or absence of a guarantee alone does not measure those connections.

2. Starting with Brunnermeier and Sannikov’s financial economy

I start with Brunnermeier and Sannikov’s (BS) economy, where households and leveraged intermediaries, called experts, hold capital. Experts are more productive, finance themselves with debt only (\(\tilde\varphi = 1\)), and choose when to pay out equity. The state variable is \(\eta = N/K\). Their Proposition 2 characterizes the Markov equilibrium through the price of capital \(q(\eta)\) and the experts’ marginal value of net worth \(\theta(\eta)\):

$$q'' = \frac{2\left(\mu^q q - q'\mu^\eta\eta\right)}{(\sigma^\eta\eta)^2},\qquad \theta'' = \frac{2\left[(\rho-r)\theta - \theta'\mu^\eta\eta\right]}{(\sigma^\eta\eta)^2},\qquad \sigma^\eta\eta = \frac{(\psi\tilde\varphi q - \eta)\,\sigma}{1-\psi\tilde\varphi q'},$$

Here \(\psi\) is determined by \(g + \underline\delta + \tilde\varphi\sigma^\theta(\sigma+\sigma^q) = 0\) when households hold capital, with \(\psi = 1\) otherwise. The boundary conditions are \(q(0) = \underline q\), \(\theta(\eta^*) = 1\), \(q'(\eta^*) = \theta'(\eta^*) = 0\), and \(\theta(0) = \infty\). I use the paper’s parameters: \(r = 5\%\), \(\rho = 6\%\), \(\underline\delta = 5\%\), \(a = \underline a = 1\), \(g = 4\%\), and \(\iota = 0\). The implementation uses the economically consistent sign for the \(\psi = 1\) condition; the inequality is reversed in the statement of Proposition 2.

Solving the model

I write \(u = \theta'/\theta\) to remove the scale of \(\theta\). Near \(\eta = 0\), the solution follows a branch with \(q - \underline q \propto \eta^{\beta}\) and \(u \approx -c/\eta\). A local expansion gives \(\beta\) and \(c\). I then use bisection to choose the branch scale so that \(q'\) and \(u\) reach zero at the same \(\eta^*\). Integration uses DOP853, with relative tolerance \(10^{-10}\) and starting point \(\eta_0 = 10^{-4}\). Changing the tolerance from \(10^{-8}\) to \(10^{-10}\), or changing \(\eta_0\) from \(10^{-4}\) to \(3\times10^{-4}\), leaves the results essentially unchanged.

Deriving a potential from the model

The potential can be derived from the stationary distribution of this one-state economy. Using \(D = (\sigma^\eta\eta)^2 p\) and \(D' = 2\mu^\eta\eta\,D/(\sigma^\eta\eta)^2\) from BS’s Appendix C, the stationary density gives \(U(\eta) = -\ln p(\eta)\). The forward equation describes a gradient flow toward \(p\), and the relative entropy \(\mathrm{KL}(p_t\,\|\,p)\) does not increase. This is the standard entropy property of a Markov process with a stationary law, and gives the Potential Descending Principle a distribution-level form in this setting.

The boundary requires care. The stationary density has an integrable singularity at zero, with \(p \sim \eta^{-0.62}\) at \(\sigma = 0.35\). The crisis well therefore extends toward \(\eta = 0\); the plotted curve begins at \(\eta = 0.5\). A formal treatment of the boundary remains an open item.

The curves are normalized to zero at the payout boundary \(\eta^*\). The potential \(U\) is nearly flat across the fire-sale region and rises steeply between \(\eta^*\) and the fire-sale boundary \(\eta^\psi\), where volatility peaks. The crisis well lies toward \(\eta \to 0\). Higher fundamental risk lowers the plateau relative to the normal well, increasing the time the economy spends in the crisis region.

3. Connecting banks’ decisions with learning about risk

My extension uses the Hamiltonian–dissipative framework developed in Wang (2026b): \(\dot X = J\nabla_X H(X,Y) + \rho\,\mathrm{diag}(0, I)X\) and \(\dot Y = -\alpha M\nabla_Y F(X,Y)\). BS supplies the optimization problem, while learning about risk supplies the dissipative dynamics. The point of coupling them is to let beliefs affect decisions and, in turn, let the consequences of those decisions affect beliefs.

The optimization problem

Let \(X = (n, \lambda)\) contain an expert’s net worth and its costate \(\lambda = \partial V/\partial n\), the marginal value of another dollar of net worth (BS’s \(\theta\)). I hold the common belief \(Y = \hat\sigma^2\) fixed when solving the continuation problem, the anticipated-utility assumption. This gives BS’s Bellman equation in the HJB form used in my coupling framework (Wang, 2026b, eq. 4), with \(V = \lambda(\eta; Y)\,n\):

$$\rho V = \max_{k,\,dc}\Big\{ dc + \mathbb{E}^{Y}\big[dV\big] \Big\},\qquad dn = \big[rn + qk\,(\mathbb{E}^{Y} r^k - r)\big]dt + qk\,(\sigma + \sigma^q)\,dZ - dc.$$

The costate follows \(d\lambda/\lambda = (\rho - r)\,dt + \sigma^\lambda dZ\), BS’s condition E; compare this with \(\dot\lambda = (\rho - \theta)\lambda\) in the growth economy. The first-order condition for capital is \(\mathbb{E}^{Y} r^k - r = -\sigma^\lambda(\sigma + \sigma^q)\), their condition EK. BS is stochastic, so the state and costate form a forward–backward system. The HJB formulation carries over, although the system is not a deterministic symplectic flow.

How beliefs change

$$\dot Y = -\alpha\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial Y},\qquad L(Y; X) = \tfrac12\big(Y - v\big)^2,\qquad v = \big(\sigma + \sigma^q\big)^2 + \text{loss news},$$

Here \(v\) is the realized variance rate of the return on capital. Agents take it as data, just as they take the realized return \(r(K)\) as data in the growth economy. The monitor uses \(\gamma\) for the learning gain \(\alpha\). The important assumption is that agents cannot distinguish fundamental risk from the price volatility they help generate, \(\sigma^q\). A value-at-risk rule based on realized volatility has a similar limitation.

How the two systems affect each other

The first direction is \(Y \to X\). Perceived risk enters the experts’ Hamiltonian through the return distribution they expect. It affects the price \(q(\eta; Y)\), the share of capital they hold \(\psi(\eta; Y)\), and their payout boundary \(\eta^*(Y)\).

The reverse direction is \(X \to Y\). The volatility agents learn from, \(\sigma^q = (q'/q)\,\sigma^\eta\eta\), depends on their own leverage and asset sales. The realized evolution of \(\eta\) also includes a forecast-error wedge: experts plan using \(\hat\sigma\), while the economy produces \(\sigma\) and realized losses.

In the notation of Theorem 1, \(dH/dt = H_Y\dot Y\) describes how changing beliefs revalue capital and net worth, while \(dL/dt = L_X\dot X - \alpha L_Y^2\) describes learning. Learning reduces forecast error, but the cross term \(L_X\dot X\) grows during a crash as falling net worth raises the volatility agents observe. A self-confirming crisis state can arise when these effects balance.

At each simulation step, I update \(\eta\) through balance-sheet accounting and clear the market at \(q(\eta_{t+dt}; Y_{t+dt})\). The temporary-equilibrium map can have several roots. I follow the stable root that is continuous with the current state.

What happens after an assumed loss?

Beliefs begin at \(\hat\sigma = 0.10\), while true risk is \(\sigma_L = 0.035\) during a calm lasting \(T\) years. True risk then remains unchanged after the loss. This lets me isolate the effect of learning from the market’s own movements.

The shock destroys capital held by intermediaries equal to a share \(\ell\) of their equity, spread over the recognition period. The deterministic path uses expected news. I record whether the model’s total-return equity index falls 30% from its 12-month high. The critical loss \(\ell^*\) is the smallest loss that produces that event; the table uses a three-month recognition period:

The figures in brackets show perceived risk when losses begin. Recognizing losses more slowly raises \(\ell^*\). With 8 years of calm and \(\gamma = 1\), the threshold rises from 3.2% when losses arrive over about five weeks to 11.9% when they arrive over two years.

The outcome changes discontinuously with \(\ell\). Beyond \(\ell^*\), the path crosses from the normal basin into the crisis basin of the coupled \((\eta, \hat\sigma)\) system, and equity falls into that state within weeks. Ma and Wang’s dynamic transition theory describes this as a catastrophic transition in the outcome. The path itself remains continuous in time, and halving the time step preserves the result.

The preceding calm matters too. A longer calm reduces \(\ell^*\) severalfold through lower perceived risk \(\hat\sigma\), a lower payout boundary, and greater leverage. Here the volatility paradox works through changing beliefs.

What I find particularly useful about the coupled model is that learning can sustain a second state. After a loss following 8 years of calm, with \(\ell = 5\%\) and \(\gamma = 1\), perceived risk settles near \(\hat\sigma = 0.091\). This is close to realized total volatility of 0.094 at \(\eta \approx 7.6\), even though true risk remains 0.035. If agents learned only from fundamental news, their beliefs would return to 0.035 regardless of \(\eta\). The persistent high-risk belief exists because \(v_t\) includes \(\sigma^q\). This is the coupling-induced bistability studied in my framework, expressed here through what agents learn from in the BS economy.

4. Connecting the model to this week’s data

The previous sections use BS’s worked example. Here I map observed data into the model and simulate what follows. This is a provisional calibration exercise. None of its outputs enters the conditions rating, and the simulated frequencies below should not be read as current U.S. crisis probabilities.

The capital measure covers six large dealer-bank holding companies: JPMorgan Chase, Bank of America, Citigroup, Wells Fargo, Goldman Sachs, and Morgan Stanley. These institutions represent only part of the financing behind AI. A low loss threshold would indicate fragility within this model of the dealers; it would not tell us how much of an AI loss would reach them. That remains the question of the loss map in section 1. A separate sector for insurers and funds, connected to banks through lending and asset prices, is part of the work ahead.

Mapping observed variables into the model

I measure each observed variable relative to its historical normal level, then apply that ratio to the corresponding normal level in the model. Let \(m(\sigma)\) be the median of \(\eta/\eta^*\) in BS’s stationary distribution, and let \(\mathrm{CR}\) denote the dealer-bank capital ratio, market equity divided by market equity plus liabilities:

$$\frac{\eta_t}{\eta^*(\hat\sigma_t)} = \min\Big\{ m(\sigma)\,\frac{\mathrm{CR}_t}{\operatorname{med}_{10y}\mathrm{CR}},\ 1\Big\},\qquad \hat\sigma_t = \sigma\,\frac{\mathrm{VIX}_t}{\operatorname{med}\mathrm{VIX}},$$
$$\sigma = 0.7\times\operatorname{med}\big(\text{realized S\&P 500 volatility}\big).$$

I set the fundamental volatility of capital to 0.099, using median realized S&P 500 volatility multiplied by 0.7. The adjustment treats equity as a leveraged claim on firms’ assets and assumes debt is about 30% of firm value. I compare the capital ratio with its median over the preceding ten years, to allow for the shift after post-2010 capital rules, and the VIX with its median since 1990. Each median uses observations through the reading date. The other BS parameters retain their worked-example values.

Estimating how quickly beliefs respond

I estimate the learning response from how option-implied variance changes with realized variance. Define \(Y = \mathrm{VIX}^2/k\), where \(k\) adjusts for the average variance premium, and let \(\mathrm{RV}\) be realized S&P 500 variance within the month:

$$\Delta Y_{t+1} = c + \gamma\,\mathrm{RV}_{t+1} - \gamma(1+\kappa)\,Y_t\,\Delta t + \varepsilon_{t+1},$$

This is the discrete form of \(\dot Y = -\gamma\,\partial L/\partial Y\) with loss function \(L = \tfrac12 (Y - v)^2 + \tfrac{\kappa}{2}(Y - \bar Y)^2\). The case \(\kappa = 0\) recovers the loss function in my coupling paper. Allowing \(\kappa > 0\) pulls beliefs toward a long-run level and raises the curvature of the loss to \(1 + \kappa\). In the linearization in Wang (2026b), this makes beliefs return to their resting level more quickly.

Does the model produce too many crises?

I check this by asking how often a month-end in a long simulation is followed by a 30% equity drawdown within a year. The share is 21% without learning, using BS with correct beliefs. With learning, it is 89% at the VIX-based speed of 3.76 and 21% at the slower speed of 0.25. The corresponding historical frequency has not yet been measured because the required bank-index history is missing. Until that comparison is made, these are model diagnostics.

At the VIX-based speed used here, the coupled economy spends most of its time in the self-confirming crisis state. Fire-sale volatility raises perceived risk, which raises the payout boundary \(\eta^*\) and lowers \(\eta/\eta^*\). That produces still more fire-sale volatility.

I don’t think these frequencies can yet be interpreted as forecasts. One possibility is that the beliefs behind balance-sheet decisions move more slowly than option-implied volatility. Another is that the fire-sale mechanism in BS’s worked example is too strong for this application. There may also be other problems with the mapping. Both the probabilities and the tipping losses need to be checked against observed outcomes before they can support claims about current U.S. fragility.

I also check the stochastic simulation with beliefs held fixed: \(\sigma = 0.1\), 1,000 paths over 50 years, and \(dt = 0.01\). The median of \(\eta/\eta^*\) is 0.976, close to 0.969 in BS’s stationary density. But the share of time below \(0.1\eta^*\) is 1.3%, compared with 2.5% in that density. The simulation therefore understates time in the deep-crisis region in this benchmark. That numerical discrepancy needs attention alongside the calibration.

6. What I’m working on next

  • Fill the gaps in the loss map, especially insurers’ funding arrangements, the holders of data-center bonds, and lending to the owners of leased facilities.
  • Build a calculator that allocates an assumed AI loss across holders and follows it through their capital to lenders. This would make the assumptions behind a bank-loss scenario explicit; it would not replace measured exposures.
  • Add insurers and funds as a separate sector, connected to banks through lending and asset prices.
  • Measure the event’s historical frequency and back-test from 1991 using the He–Kelly–Manela capital ratio. The scripts are in place.
  • Solve BS using U.S. estimates of intermediary leverage and fire-sale discounts, then test whether the model can match both the learning response and observed crisis frequency.
  • Estimate how quickly balance-sheet beliefs change, using banks’ reported value-at-risk or slower measures such as news-implied volatility (Manela and Moreira, 2017).
  • Build a bank-equity total-return event series to align the empirical target with the model, and retain real-time vintages of the credit ratios. The monitor currently tracks price-index closes.
  • Compare with a Bayesian regime-learning benchmark in the spirit of Boz and Mendoza (2014), and with the log-utility BS (2016) model, where the price ODE is first order.
  • Prove the fold of the temporary-equilibrium map and characterize \(\ell^*\) analytically in terms of \(\gamma\), \(\hat\sigma\) and \(q'(\eta)\).
